Offshore to Onshore: Lessons from 6 Years in Marine HSE

READ ON LINKEDIN ↗

DR NADEEM UKAYE · 10 MIN · DOSEOFHSE.COM · PUBLISHED 2026-11-07

Between 2010 and 2016 I worked offshore safety, first in marine and drilling support, and the work carried me through more than one flag in that period: Iran, Qatar, Dubai, Congo and Angola, where those years ended. Since 2016 I have led regional health, safety and environment from Abu Dhabi for facilities across several regions. Onshore taught me new systems. Offshore taught me discipline, and this piece is about the part worth carrying back.

Offshore strips ambiguity fast. On a rig, the environment is small and absolute: weather, water depth, crane capacity, deck space, and a finite set of people who must all get home. There is no room to spread risk across quarters. That constraint produces habits, and these are the ones I carried ashore.

Permits are real. On marine and drilling work, the permit to work system is not paperwork, it is the choreography that keeps simultaneous operations from killing each other. Hot work above, entry below, lifting across: every overlapping job is a negotiation that somebody documented. Onshore sites often soften this into a form-filling exercise. Where I could, I pushed the offshore standard: if the permit cannot be read as a story of who is doing what, where and when, it is a ticket, not a control.

Risk assessment before arrival, not after the near miss. Offshore, every non-routine task got a hazard study, because the distance to help makes improvisation expensive. HAZID and HIRA language lives in your bones out there. Onshore teams have more slack, and slack is where exposure grows. Planning the task before mobilising costs hours and saves careers.

Confined spaces deserve their fear. The protocols are strict on paper everywhere, and offshore they are strict in muscle memory: gas test, isolation, standby attendant, rescue plan before anyone's foot moves. When I later reviewed onshore confined space practices, the gap was rarely knowledge. It was confidence culture: the belief that familiarity makes an exception safe. Familiarity makes exceptions familiar, that is all.

Pressure is not an argument for skipping steps. Operational pressure offshore is constant and extreme at times, and the schedule always argues for shortcuts. What held was never motivation. It was the quiet authority of a supervisor who said stop, and a system that backed the stop. That is the entire culture question in one sentence.

Finally, fatigue. Offshore rotations are built around rest because the sea does not negotiate. Onshore operations often treat fatigue as a personal matter. It is an operational hazard, and the best onshore organisations I work with now schedule and monitor it with the seriousness offshore taught me.

I keep those six years close. They were hard, often far from home, and completely formative. Everything I led afterwards stood on that base. If your career has touched offshore work, name it in your practice. The industry's onshore side is safer for it.
