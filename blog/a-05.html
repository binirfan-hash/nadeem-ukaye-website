The Business Case for Predictive Safety: ROI Beyond the Incident Rate

READ ON LINKEDIN ↗

DR NADEEM UKAYE · 11 MIN · DOSEOFHSE.COM · PUBLISHED 2026-11-07

Ask a board what safety costs and you will get a number. Ask what not having it costs, and most still shrug, because the ledger of an injury hides its size across five different accounts. I want to make that ledger visible, because predictive safety is not a technology story, it is a money story told honestly.

Start with the visible account: insurance and claims. Premiums track the record. When your injury frequency measures fall hard and stay down, as our measures did in recent years, falling to around four fifths below earlier levels with zero recorded lost-time incidents at our best, you are not just safer, you are a cheaper risk to carry. Underwriters read lagging indicators. Predictive work aims at the ones that lead, which is exactly what insurers are beginning to charge for.

Direct incident costs come next: medical care, equipment damage, downtime, investigation time, replacement labour, fines. These are the numbers finance already sees. Two layers usually get missed. The indirect ones: schedule slips when a crew is stood down, a client audit that turns hostile, management attention borrowed from growth and spent on recovery. And the human ones, which no dashboard prices and every leader should still weigh first: a colleague who does not return to work the same, a family that waits by a phone.

Morale and retention sit in the same ledger. Operational people notice who gets hurt and what the company did about it. In regions where skilled trades have choices, a visible, modern safety system is a recruiting asset. It reads as respect, because it is.

Compliance is a floor with a price tag attached. Regulatory attention, client audits and certification all consume leadership hours, and the worst version of that cost is reactive: scrambling before an audit. A governance system that already knows its own weak signals walks into audits calm. That calm has cash value.

So what does predictive safety add in money terms? Three returns, in my experience. It moves spend upstream: reading patterns in near misses, maintenance backlogs, permit timings and environmental data costs far less than post-incident everything. It targets effort: instead of inspecting everything equally, leaders inspect where risk is rising, which means the same headcount buys more protection. And it shortens learning loops: a model that flags a correlation in March gives you a fix before the quarter closes.

What it does not do is remove leadership. Tools rank, humans decide, and the ROI arrives only when a named leader acts on the reading the way they would act on a permit violation they saw with their own eyes.

If you carry a budget, you already accept that prevention has a return. The question predictive safety answers is precisely which prevention, and when. I would call that hygiene in the financial sense of the word.

Start with one line of your own data, one number the board already watches, and trace what it would be worth if you saw it two months early. That trace is the business case, written by your own operations.
